ADVANCED MATH PROJECT · STUDENT DATA ANALYSIS

Social Environment and Academic Performance

Exploring how students’ perceived friend influence and time spent with friends are associated with their academic performance.

Research question and motivation

How are students’ perceived levels of friend influence and their daily time spent with friends associated with academic performance?

University life involves much more than attending classes and preparing for exams. Students also spend time with friends, exchange ideas, and participate in social activities. These interactions are part of a student's social environment and may be related to their study experience. However, an association between social experiences and academic results is not necessarily simple or positive or negative.

This project focuses on two specific aspects of that environment: how strongly students feel influenced by friends and how much time they report spending with friends each day. Academic performance is represented by an ordered GPA category. The goal is to investigate patterns in the data, not to decide whether having friends is 'good' or 'bad' for grades.

Importantly, the friend-influence question measures the reported degree of influence; it does not say whether that influence was supportive, distracting, positive, or negative. The results are therefore interpreted as associations rather than effects.

Dataset and variables

The analysis uses 2,170 student records from a publicly available dataset associated with a 2025 Data in Brief article about students at the University of Education, Vietnam National University, Hanoi. The dataset is distributed through Mendeley Data in an Excel workbook named Database paper.xlsx.

Three variables are selected from the wider dataset. All three are recorded as ordered categories coded from 1 to 5, rather than as precise continuous measurements:

Statistical hypotheses: H₀ and H₁

The chi-square test of independence examines whether the distribution of GPA categories differs across the five reported levels of friend influence. Before interpreting the test, we state two competing hypotheses:

H₀ — Null hypothesis

Perceived friend influence and GPA category are independent. In the population represented by the sample, there is no association between these two categorical variables.

H₁ — Alternative hypothesis

Perceived friend influence and GPA category are not independent. There is an association between these two categorical variables.

Decision and interpretation

Using a conventional significance level of α = 0.05, the chi-square test gives χ² ≈ 71.48 with 16 degrees of freedom and p ≈ 5.5 × 10⁻⁹. Because p is smaller than 0.05, we reject H₀ in favor of evidence for an association. A permutation test with 2,000 shuffles also gives a small p-value (approximately 0.00050).

Rejecting H₀ does not mean that the relationship is strong or causal. Cramér’s V ≈ 0.091 indicates a weak association, and the study cannot establish whether friends cause changes in GPA. These hypotheses concern the friend-influence versus GPA chi-square analysis; the time-with-friends heatmap is a descriptive exploration rather than a separate hypothesis test.

Analytical approach

1. Descriptive comparison

First, students are grouped by their reported friend-influence category. Within each group, the percentage of students in each GPA category is calculated. Using percentages rather than raw counts makes groups of different sizes easier to compare. The stacked bar chart presents the complete GPA distribution, while a second chart combines GPA categories 4 and 5 to give a simpler view of the proportion with relatively higher GPA.

2. Chi-square test and association strength

A chi-square test of independence compares the observed counts in the five-by-five table of friend influence and GPA with the counts expected if the two categorical variables were independent. A small p-value indicates that the observed pattern would be unusual under the independence assumption. It does not tell us whether the relationship is large or important in practice.

For that reason, the analysis also reports Cramér's V, a measure of association strength for categorical variables. Its value is close to zero here, indicating that the detected association is weak.

3. Permutation check

Some combinations of friend influence and GPA contain relatively few students, which makes the usual chi-square approximation less dependable. To provide an additional check, the analysis randomly shuffles GPA labels across students 2,000 times, recalculating the chi-square statistic after each shuffle. This creates a reference distribution for a situation in which the observed link between the variables has been broken.

The permutation p-value is the proportion of shuffled results at least as extreme as the observed result, using a small adjustment to avoid reporting zero. With 2,000 shuffles, the smallest reportable adjusted p-value is approximately 0.00050.

4. Exploring two aspects of social environment together

Finally, a heatmap shows the percentage of students in GPA categories 4 or 5 for each combination of friend-influence category and time-with-friends category. The number of students in each combination is shown inside the cell. Cells with fewer than 20 observations are hidden because percentages from very small groups can be unstable. This heatmap is descriptive; it is not a separate statistical test proving a joint effect.

The chi-square test comparing all five friend-influence levels with all five GPA categories produced χ² ≈ 71.48 with 16 degrees of freedom. Its approximate p-value was 5.5 × 10⁻⁹. This provides statistical evidence that the full distribution of GPA categories is not identical across the friend-influence groups in this sample.

However, Cramér's V ≈ 0.091 shows that the association is weak. This distinction matters: a statistically detectable pattern does not necessarily mean that friend influence is a strong predictor of a student's academic performance.

There is also a technical caution. Six of the 25 expected cell counts in the chi-square table were below five, so the usual chi-square p-value should be interpreted carefully. The additional permutation test returned an adjusted p-value of approximately 0.00050 (based on 2,000 shuffles), which supports the conclusion that the observed five-category association is unlikely under independence.

When the GPA categories are simplified into “higher GPA” (categories 4 and 5) versus all others, the percentages are fairly similar in the three largest friend-influence groups. Therefore, the evidence should not be described as showing that stronger friend influence consistently leads to higher or lower grades.

Discussion, interpretation, and limitations

The main conclusion is that perceived friend influence and GPA categories are statistically associated, but only weakly in this sample. The chi-square and permutation tests detect differences in the full category distributions, while Cramér's V indicates that the overall relationship is small. The higher-GPA bar chart also shows why statistical significance should not be confused with a large or clear practical effect.

The heatmap suggests that higher-GPA percentages vary between some combinations of friend influence and time with friends. Nevertheless, a cell percentage is not an estimate of what would happen if a student changed how much time they spent with friends. A difference between two groups could also reflect other characteristics of those students.

Why the results require caution

  • No causal conclusion: this is observational data, not an experiment. The analysis cannot establish that friends cause GPA to rise or fall.
  • Self-reported categories: friend influence and time spent with friends are recorded as broad categories, which lose detail and may be affected by reporting differences.
  • Unequal group sizes: very low friend-influence categories contain far fewer students than moderate, quite, or very influential categories. Their percentages are less stable.
  • Small expected counts: six cells in the chi-square analysis have expected counts below five; the permutation test is used as an additional check.
  • Other possible explanations: study habits, academic motivation, family circumstances, course demands, and other unmeasured factors may be associated with both social experiences and GPA.
  • Limited generalizability: the dataset comes from a particular student population and should not automatically be treated as representative of all university students.

Overall, the project illustrates how several statistical and visual approaches can reveal different aspects of the same question. It also shows why the size, reliability, and interpretation of an association are as important as whether a p-value is small.

Reproducibility and project files

The analysis was carried out in Python using a Jupyter notebook in Google Colab. The notebook loads the Excel dataset, creates the descriptive tables, performs the chi-square and permutation analyses, and exports the three figures as PDF files. The HTML file provides a public-facing explanation of the research question, approach, and results.

To reproduce the results, download Database paper.xlsx from the linked Mendeley Data record, open the notebook in Google Colab, upload the workbook into the notebook session, and run the cells in order. The notebook refers to the original column names, including InfuenceF_Friends.

The public repository contains the Python notebook, the three original PDF figures, the website source, and a README with the data source and reproduction instructions.
